Essay Cheat Sheet

Write essays that sound like you, not like a chatbot.

Admissions officers read thousands of essays a season. They reward specificity, voice, and restraint — not eloquence for its own sake.

The structure that works

Introduction (10–15%)

Open with a concrete scene, an image, or a small contradiction. Not a thesis. Not "ever since I was a child." Drop the reader into a moment.

Body (70%)

Trace one or two specific experiences that shaped how you see nursing. Show — through action, dialogue, sensory detail. Connect each scene to a value or insight that matters.

Conclusion (15–20%)

Land on what you'll bring to the program and the kind of nurse you want to become. Concrete and forward-looking. No flowery summary.

Three skeleton essays you can fork

Don't copy — adapt. These are scaffolds, not templates.

A · "Why nursing"

  1. Open with a specific moment in a clinical or care setting — a hospital waiting room, a home visit, a school nurse interaction. One sensory detail (the smell of bleach, the sound of a heart monitor, the weight of someone's hand).
  2. Zoom out to what that moment revealed — about people under stress, about the difference a calm presence makes, about the gap between knowing and helping.
  3. Connect to two or three concrete experiences since that moment that confirmed the path: volunteering, a class, a book, a conversation with a nurse.
  4. Name the kind of nurse you want to become. Specific specialty if you have one, or specific qualities you want to embody.
  5. Close with one image that ties back to the opening. Same scene, but you in it differently.

B · "Service / character"

  1. Start in the middle of doing something hard for someone else — not the inciting incident, but the moment of friction. The person you're helping doesn't behave how you expected.
  2. Describe what you actually did, including what didn't work. Show the mistake or the misunderstanding.
  3. Show what you learned about humility, listening, or patience — through one specific change in how you acted next time.
  4. Don't make yourself the hero. Avoid framing where you "saved" anyone. Focus on the relationship and the work, not the rescue.
  5. Tie to nursing: caring is a craft, not just a feeling.

C · "Adversity"

  1. Be specific about the adversity. Don't write generally about hardship. One thing that happened, briefly described.
  2. Spend most of the essay on what you did about it — not on the suffering. The reader will infer the weight; you don't have to lean on it.
  3. Show one or two strategies you developed — practical, repeatable habits, not a vague resilience.
  4. Don't tie everything up with a bow. It's fine to admit something is still hard. Honesty beats catharsis.
  5. Connect to nursing: the strategies you developed are the same strategies that help under clinical pressure.

Common nursing-school prompts

  • Why nursing? Most schools ask this directly. Specific origin moment + recent confirmation + future intent.
  • Why this school? Name one course, one professor, one program, one clinical partner. Generic praise is fatal.
  • Tell us about a challenge. Pick a small, concrete challenge over a sweeping one. Show problem-solving, not suffering.
  • How will you contribute to diversity? Avoid the trap of writing about yourself as a category. Write about how your background shapes how you see specific things others might miss.
  • Describe a meaningful relationship with a patient or person you cared for. Specific. Not the whole life story. One conversation, one moment.

Anti-patterns to delete

The savior narrative

"I want to help the less fortunate." This positions you above the people you care for. Admissions officers see it constantly and dislike it. Write about being changed by people, not about rescuing them.

The chatbot voice

Watch for phrases like "navigate the complexities," "profound impact," "in today's rapidly changing world," "make a difference." If you used Claude or ChatGPT to draft, run the result through the AI Helper "Rewrite to remove AI-sounding phrasing" before you submit.

Generic origin stories

"Ever since I was a child." "From a young age." Skip them. Drop the reader into a specific moment instead — the reader doesn't care that you've wanted this for a long time; they care that you can write about a specific scene with specific people.

Adjective stuffing

"Passionate, dedicated, compassionate, hardworking." Adjectives without evidence are noise. If you're tempted to write one, replace it with the action or observation that proves it instead.

The revision protocol

  1. Draft fast, then walk away for 48 hours. Your future self is a better editor than your present self.
  2. Read it aloud. If you stumble, the reader will too. Trim what you stumble on.
  3. Cut 10% of the word count. Whatever you cut, the essay is almost always better for it.
  4. Ask one teacher or trusted adult to read it. Ask: "What do you remember 24 hours after reading this?" If they can't answer, the essay isn't memorable yet.
  5. Run a plagiarism + AI-detection pass. Even if you wrote every word, some scanners flag standard phrasing as AI. Rephrase anything that scores high.
  6. Sleep on it. Submit in the morning. Never submit at 11:55 PM.
