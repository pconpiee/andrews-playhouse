Interview Prep

Stay composed. Think out loud. Be specific.

Interviews don't reward perfect answers — they reward the way you reason, listen, and stay grounded under mild pressure.

Two formats you'll meet

Traditional interview

One interviewer, 20–45 minutes. Conversational. They want to know who you are and why this program.

Most common questions: Tell me about yourself · Why nursing? · Why this school? · What's the hardest thing about being a nurse? · A time you handled a difficult situation · Strengths / weaknesses · A patient or person you helped.

Multiple Mini Interview (MMI)

6–10 short stations, ~8 minutes each, different evaluator at each. Used by many nursing schools and most med schools. More objective than traditional. MMI in nursing admissions

Question types: personal · ethical dilemma · patient scenario · policy · role-play.

The STAR method (for traditional interviews)

When asked a behavioral question ("tell me about a time when..."), structure your answer:

  • S — Situation. One sentence: where, when, who.
  • T — Task. One sentence: what you needed to do.
  • A — Action. Three to five sentences: what you actually did, step by step.
  • R — Result. One sentence: what happened, what you learned.

Most candidates spend too much time on Situation and Task. Spend the most time on Action — it's the only part the interviewer can score.

How to attack an MMI station

  1. Read the prompt twice. You usually have 2 minutes outside the room to think.
  2. Identify the type: personal / ethical / scenario / policy / role-play. Each has a different structure.
  3. For ethical dilemmas: name the values in tension (e.g. patient autonomy vs. safety), then walk through your reasoning, then state your decision and acknowledge the cost.
  4. For patient scenarios: what do you not know? What questions would you ask? What is the immediate safety concern? Then act.
  5. For policy: three considerations — patient outcomes, equity / access, system feasibility. Don't pretend to have a clean answer; show you've thought about trade-offs.
  6. For role-play: be present with the actor, not performing for the evaluator. Listen, respond, don't try to be clever.
  7. Leave 30 seconds at the end to summarize. "So to recap, my main concern is X; the action I'd take is Y; the trade-off I'm accepting is Z."

12 sample MMI prompts (with how to approach them)

1 — Personal

What is one weakness that would hold you back as a nurse?

Approach: name a real weakness (not "I work too hard"), describe one specific situation it showed up in, describe the concrete practice you've built to manage it, and acknowledge it'll still be work.

2 — Ethical dilemma

A friend tells you they cheated on an exam. What do you do?

Approach: values in tension (loyalty vs. integrity of the profession). Talk to the friend first. Don't escalate without giving them the chance to come forward. If they refuse and the cheating affected others' grades, report. State your decision and the cost.

3 — Patient scenario

An elderly patient refuses a medication you know would help them. What do you do?

Approach: name autonomy as the core value. Ask why they're refusing — fear, side effect, cost, religious reason. Educate without coercing. Document the refusal. Involve family / care team if appropriate. Respect the patient's choice while ensuring informed consent.

4 — Policy

Should nurses be allowed to refuse to perform a procedure on religious grounds?

Approach: tension between conscience and patient access. Consider transfer protocols (the patient's care continues with another provider). Equity concern in rural areas with one nurse. Argue for a principled middle ground.

5 — Role-play

A patient's family member is angry that their loved one has been waiting 3 hours. They start raising their voice. Respond.

Approach: acknowledge their feelings first ("I can see you've been waiting a long time and you're frustrated. I would be too."). Don't defend the system. Explain what you can do now. Give a concrete next step.

6 — Personal

Tell me about a time you failed at something important.

Approach: STAR. Pick a real failure with stakes. Spend most time on what you learned and what you did differently next time. Avoid framing it as a humble-brag ("I failed because I cared too much").

7 — Ethical dilemma

You discover a colleague made a small medication error and didn't report it. What do you do?

Approach: patient safety is non-negotiable. Approach the colleague first. If they won't report, you must. Reporting is not betrayal; it's protecting future patients.

8 — Patient scenario

A patient tells you they're afraid of dying. What do you say?

Approach: sit down. Don't deflect with reassurance. Ask what specifically they're afraid of. Listen. Reflect back what you heard. Offer to call a chaplain, family, or palliative-care specialist. Don't pretend to have answers you don't have.

9 — Policy

Should hospitals require COVID vaccination for staff?

Approach: patient protection (especially immunocompromised), public health duty of healthcare workers, individual liberty, religious exemption. State your position clearly and name what you're trading off.

10 — Role-play

A teenager tells you in confidence they're being abused at home. They beg you not to tell anyone.

Approach: mandatory reporting laws override confidentiality in this situation in the US. Be honest with them — "I can't keep this secret because keeping you safe matters more than keeping it private." Walk them through who you'll tell and what happens next. Stay with them.

11 — Personal

Why are you applying to study in the United States specifically, rather than at home?

Approach: be honest. Quality of clinical training, access to specific specialties, scholarship opportunity. Mention what you plan to bring back or contribute to. Avoid disparaging your home country's healthcare system.

12 — Ethical dilemma

A patient asks you what religion you are. Do you answer?

Approach: brief honest answer is usually fine; pivot back to them ("Why do you ask?"). Be careful about proselytizing — never appropriate from a clinical position. If the patient is seeking spiritual care, refer to chaplaincy services.

Remote interviews (Zoom)

  • Camera at eye level. Stack books under the laptop if needed.
  • Look at the camera lens when speaking, not at the interviewer's face on the screen. Practice this — it feels unnatural.
  • Lighting in front of you, not behind. A window in front is great. A window behind silhouettes you.
  • Wired ethernet if possible. Most Addis WiFi can drop mid-interview.
  • Have a backup phone hotspot set up and ready.
  • Test the platform (Zoom, Teams, Spark, whatever) the day before.
  • Notes are usually allowed for MMI prompts but not for traditional interviews. Confirm with the school.
  • Dress like you're in person from the waist up.

Mock-interview protocol

  1. Do at least 3 mocks before any real interview. Real interviewers, not yourself in a mirror.
  2. Record every mock. Watch it back the next day.
  3. For each mock, ask the interviewer to score you on: structure of reasoning, communication clarity, composure under follow-up, and warmth.
  4. For MMI practice, use the AI Helper "Generate 10 MMI questions and score my answers" to drill alone, then do live mocks with a person.
