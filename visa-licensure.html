Visa, Licensure & Long-Term Path

The legal road from Addis to a US nursing license — and a green card after.

The good news first: registered nurses are on the US Department of Labor "Schedule A" list, which means US employers can sponsor an employment-based green card for you without the usual labor-certification process. The path is real and the destination is reachable. This page covers the visa, the licensure exam, the state-by-state rules that catch people out, and the long-term immigration options.

The whole road in seven steps

  1. Get into a US-accredited BSN program. The Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) must be accredited by the Commission on Collegiate Nursing Education (CCNE) or the Accreditation Commission for Education in Nursing (ACEN). All 30 schools on this site meet this bar.
  2. Get the F-1 student visa at the U.S. Embassy in Addis Ababa.
  3. Study for four years and graduate.
  4. Sit the licensure exam, the NCLEX-RN. Pass it to be eligible to apply for a US state nursing license.
  5. Pick the state where you'll be licensed — different US states have different rules for F-1 graduates. This decision should be made before you pick the school, not after.
  6. Use Optional Practical Training (OPT) — 12 months of work authorization after graduation — to start your first nursing job at a US hospital.
  7. Move to a permanent visa. Either an H-1B (lottery-based, harder) or an employer-sponsored EB-3 green card via the Schedule A fast-track (easier for nurses than for almost any other field).

F-1 student visa

The F-1 is the standard US visa for full-time academic study. To get one you need three things in this order: an admission letter from a US-accredited school, a Form I-20 from that school (the certificate of eligibility), and a successful visa interview at the U.S. Embassy in Addis Ababa.

How it works

  • The I-20 is the school's certification that you are an enrolled, full-time international student. The school's Designated School Official (DSO) issues it after you've documented Year-1 funding.
  • SEVIS is the US database that tracks all international students. You pay the SEVIS I-901 fee ($350) before your visa interview.
  • The DS-160 is the visa application form you complete online before the interview.
  • The MRV fee ($185 as of 2025; confirm before paying) is the application fee paid to the US Embassy.
  • The interview itself is short — usually 2 to 5 minutes — and the consular officer is looking for three things: that you are a genuine student, that you have the funds, and that you intend to return to Ethiopia after your studies.

Proof of funds & sponsor traps

Before issuing the I-20, the school will require documented proof that you can pay for the first year — tuition, fees, room, board, and living expenses. They will also want a credible plan for Years 2 through 4, even though documentation isn't legally required upfront. Acceptable evidence:

  • Family bank statements showing liquid funds in the family's name.
  • Scholarship award letters from the school or external sponsors.
  • A sponsor affidavit — a legal pledge by a person or organization to financially support you.

Affidavits of support are legally binding for the sponsor.

When a relative or organization signs a Form I-134 Affidavit of Support (or the more serious Form I-864 used in green-card contexts), they are entering a legal commitment to financially support you. For the I-864 in particular, the sponsor's obligation can last for years and can be enforced by the US government. Before asking a relative to sponsor you, both you and they should fully understand what they are signing. USCIS I-134

The F-1 visa interview at the U.S. Embassy in Addis

Ethiopia has historically had F-1 refusal rates above the global average, mostly driven by "Section 214(b)" refusals — the consular officer is not convinced the applicant intends to return home. The single most important thing to demonstrate is genuine ties to Ethiopia.

What to bring

  • Passport (valid for 6+ months past expected travel)
  • I-20 from the school
  • SEVIS I-901 fee receipt
  • DS-160 confirmation page (with barcode)
  • MRV fee receipt
  • Original admission letter
  • Financial documents (bank statements, scholarship letters, sponsor affidavits)
  • Academic records (transcripts, TOEFL/SAT scores)
  • Ethiopian ID

What the consular officer will ask

  • Why this school? Have a specific, factual answer — a course, a faculty member, a program detail.
  • Why nursing? Short, personal, honest.
  • How will you pay for it? Match the answer to your I-20 documentation precisely.
  • What will you do after you graduate? The "right" answer is some version of "return to Ethiopia to apply what I've learned" — even if your real plan involves H-1B and a green card later, your stated intent at the F-1 stage must be to return home. This is not a trick; it is the legal standard for the F-1 visa.

OPT — what you can do for 12 months after graduation

Optional Practical Training (OPT) is temporary US work authorization that allows F-1 students to work in their field of study for up to 12 months after graduation. For nursing students, this is the window during which you start your first US nursing job — typically at the hospital your clinical rotations were affiliated with.

Traditional nursing is NOT on the STEM-OPT list.

Most science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) majors get an additional 24-month OPT extension. Traditional BSN is not on that list, so nursing graduates get the standard 12 months only. To stay in the US beyond that, you'll need either an H-1B work visa or — more practically — an employer-sponsored green card under Schedule A. USCIS STEM OPT

NCLEX-RN — the national licensure exam

The National Council Licensure Examination for Registered Nurses (NCLEX-RN) is the single US licensure exam every registered nurse must pass to practice. It is administered by the National Council of State Boards of Nursing (NCSBN). The exam is computer-adaptive, taken at Pearson VUE test centers, and ranges from 75 to 150 questions depending on how you perform.

  • You apply to a US state Board of Nursing first, not to the NCLEX directly. The state board confirms your eligibility, then NCSBN issues your "Authorization to Test."
  • Most US-accredited BSN graduates pass NCLEX on first attempt — the national first-attempt rate is around 80–90%.
  • The exam costs $200 plus state-board fees.
  • You can take it during your OPT year, which is the standard path for international graduates.

State Boards of Nursing — the trap by state

Passing the NCLEX-RN qualifies you to apply for licensure — but each US state Board of Nursing (BON) sets its own rules. For F-1 graduates, the differences are large and often unobvious.

Pick the state before you pick the school.

It is possible to graduate from a CCNE-accredited BSN program, pass the NCLEX, and still be unable to obtain a US state nursing license — because of state-specific rules around your visa status. This is the single most common avoidable mistake international nursing students make.

The big variations

  • Social Security Number (SSN) requirement. Texas, Alabama, and several other state BONs require an SSN to be issued a license. F-1 students can only obtain an SSN if they have on-campus employment lined up — and once your OPT begins, you typically have a narrow window (60–90 days) to find a job that issues you an SSN. Plan ahead.
  • English-language requirements. California's Board of Registered Nursing requires foreign-educated nurses to demonstrate English proficiency. US-trained graduates are generally exempt, but confirm with California BRN.
  • Background check and fingerprint requirements. All states require these, but California, New York, and several others have additional documentation steps for foreign-born applicants.
  • Visa-status restrictions. A handful of state BONs have at times restricted licensure for non-immigrant visa holders. This is an evolving area; always check the current rules.

Where to check

Each state Board of Nursing has a website listing licensure requirements. Before committing to a school, search "[state name] Board of Nursing licensure international students" and read the requirements for your target state directly. The NCSBN keeps a directory:

CGFNS & the VisaScreen step

The Commission on Graduates of Foreign Nursing Schools (CGFNS) handles two distinct products that are easy to confuse.

  • CGFNS Certification is for nurses trained outside the US who want to be licensed in the US. If your BSN is from a US-accredited program, you skip this entirely.
  • CGFNS VisaScreen is a separate certificate required by US immigration for nurses applying for an H-1B work visa or an employment-based green card — regardless of where you were trained. Even US-educated F-1 graduates need a VisaScreen to convert from OPT to a longer-term work visa. Plan for it during your OPT year; the process takes 3–6 months and costs ~$540.

Schedule A — the green card on a fast track

The single most important long-term immigration fact for international nursing students: registered nurses are listed as a "Schedule A" occupation by the US Department of Labor. This means employers can sponsor a green card for you under the Employment-Based Third Preference (EB-3) category without going through the usual labor-certification (PERM) process. That eliminates 12–18 months of bureaucracy compared with sponsorship for almost any other professional field.

  • The employer files a Form I-140 Immigrant Petition directly, with the Schedule A designation.
  • You can apply for adjustment of status from OPT or H-1B once a visa number is available for your country.
  • The wait times depend on your country of birth, not your country of citizenship. Ethiopia is generally not subject to per-country backlogs the way India and China are, which means the EB-3 wait can be 1–3 years rather than a decade.
  • Many US hospitals actively sponsor international nurses for EB-3. When choosing where to do your OPT year, prioritize hospital systems known for sponsoring (large urban systems and rural-shortage area employers).

This is the difference between "nursing in America for a few years" and "nursing in America permanently if you want." It is the most underused piece of information in the international-nursing playbook.

H-1B as a bridge

The H-1B is a 3-year (renewable to 6) US work visa for "specialty occupations." Registered nurses can sometimes qualify if the role requires a bachelor's degree (most staff RN roles in the US still officially require only an associate degree, which complicates H-1B eligibility for nurses). The H-1B is also lottery-based — applications are filed in March each year and only a fraction are selected.

For most nursing graduates, the H-1B is the harder path; the EB-3 green card via Schedule A is the easier path. Some employers run both processes in parallel. USCIS H-1B

Currency & sponsor commitments

The Ethiopian birr has lost roughly half its value against the US dollar in the past two years, and this trend is structural rather than temporary. A sponsor's commitment in birr today may deliver materially less in US dollars by next year.

  • Whenever possible, denominate sponsor commitments in US dollars, not birr.
  • Plan for currency cushion. If your aid plan assumes ETB 1 = USD 0.018 today, model what happens at USD 0.012 in two years.
  • Diaspora bank accounts (US-dollar-denominated savings held in the US by family in the diaspora, if available) are more resilient than birr-denominated holdings for funding US tuition.
  • Schools that are full-need-met protect you from currency risk in a way that schools requiring substantial family contribution do not.
